# i3League — Stage 4: ITERATE
### Guiding Question: Can we build and test the critical assumption?

---

## 1. MVP Thinking — Minimum Viable Product / Minimum Testable Version

**What is it?**
In i3League, think of your MVP as the smallest version you can build that lets you learn something important. It does not need to be a finished product.

**Why does it matter?**
You don't need to build the full, finished version of your idea to learn something valuable. Building small and testing early saves time and helps you avoid wasting effort on the wrong details.

**Example**
Instead of building a full working app, you sketch three screens on paper to see if students understand how the hallway-alert idea would work.

**Common mistake**
Trying to build a polished, complete version before testing whether the core idea even works.

**Try this**
Ask: what is the smallest version of my idea that would still let me test my critical assumption?

**Key takeaway**
Build the smallest thing that teaches you the most.

**Think about it**
Are you building more than you actually need to test your assumption right now?

**Use this in your project**
Describe your MVP in your Test Plan, and explain what it's designed to test.

---

## 2. Prototype Fidelity — Build the Right Level

**What is it?**
Fidelity means how close your prototype is to the real, finished thing. A low-fidelity prototype might be paper sketches; a high-fidelity one might be a working app. Both are legitimate — the right choice depends on what you're testing.

**Why does it matter?**
Building higher fidelity than you need wastes time. Building lower fidelity than you need might not give you a real answer. Matching fidelity to your test question keeps you efficient.

**Example**
If you're testing whether people understand your idea, a paper sketch might be enough. If you're testing whether people will actually use it repeatedly, you may need something more functional.

**Common mistake**
Assuming a "real" prototype always means a finished, polished, working product.

**Try this**
Decide: does your test question need a sketch, a clickable mockup, a physical model, or a working version? Choose the simplest option that still answers it.

**Key takeaway**
Build only as real as you need to answer your question.

**Think about it**
Could you test your assumption with something simpler than what you're currently planning to build?

**Use this in your project**
Note your chosen prototype fidelity and why it fits your test question in your Test Plan.

---

## 3. Experiment Design Basics

**What is it?**
Experiment design means setting up a test so you actually know what caused the result — by changing one important thing at a time and paying attention to what happens.

**Why does it matter?**
If you change several things at once, you won't know which change actually caused the result you saw. A clear, simple design keeps your test meaningful.

**Example**
Testing two different versions of a hallway sign — one with color, one with text only — to see which one people notice faster, while keeping everything else about the test the same.

**Common mistake**
Changing multiple things at once and then not being able to tell what actually made the difference.

**Try this**
Identify the one thing your test is meant to check, and make sure everything else about the test stays the same.

**Key takeaway**
Change one thing, so you know what taught you something.

**Think about it**
Is there more than one variable changing in your planned test?

**Use this in your project**
Describe what single thing you're testing in your Test Plan.

---

## 4. Test Questions & Success Criteria

**What is it?**
Before testing, you should be able to clearly state what exactly you're trying to learn, and what result would count as support — or a challenge — to your assumption.

**Why does it matter?**
Without a clear question and a clear bar for success, almost any result can be interpreted as "it worked." That makes testing meaningless.

**Example**
Test question: "Will students notice and understand the hallway alert within 5 seconds?" Success criteria: "At least 8 of 10 students correctly explain what the alert means within 5 seconds."

**Common mistake**
Testing something vaguely and then deciding afterward whether it "felt like" it worked. ("We showed it to five friends and they liked it.")

**Try this**
Write your test question and a specific, measurable success criteria before you run your test — not after.

**Key takeaway**
Decide what counts as success before you test, not after.

**Think about it**
If your test succeeded and you weren't sure why, would you actually know what to do next?

**Use this in your project**
Add your test question and success criteria to your Test Plan before testing.

## 5. Usability Basics

**What is it?**
Usability means how easy and clear your solution is for a real person to actually use — without confusion, without needing extra instructions, and without frustration.

**Why does it matter?**
A great idea that's confusing or frustrating to use won't get adopted, no matter how good the underlying concept is.

**Example**
If students can't figure out what a hallway alert icon means without being told, the design isn't usable yet — even if the underlying idea is solid.

**Common mistake**
Assuming something is easy to use because it makes sense to you, the person who built it.

**Try this**
Hand your prototype to someone unfamiliar with the project and watch them try to use it without help.

**Key takeaway**
Unexpected explanation is a usability signal.

**Think about it**
Did the person testing your prototype need you to explain anything?

**Use this in your project**
Note any usability confusion you observe in your Feedback Log.

## 6. Observation vs. Opinion

**What is it?**
An observation is what you actually saw someone do. An opinion is what someone says they think or feel about it. Both are useful, but they're different kinds of evidence and shouldn't be confused.

**Why does it matter?**
People sometimes say they "like" something but then don't actually use it the way they claimed they would. Watching what people do is often more reliable than only asking what they think.

**Example**
Opinion: "I think this is a cool idea." Observation: the same person paused, looked confused, and asked a friend what to do next.

**Common mistake**
Recording only positive opinions ("they said they liked it") and skipping what you actually observed them doing.

**Try this**
During your next test, write down two columns: what you observed people doing, and what they said afterward.

**Key takeaway**
Watch what people do — don't just listen to what they say.

**Think about it**
Is there a moment where what someone said didn't match what they actually did?

**Use this in your project**
Record both observations and opinions separately in your Feedback Log.

---

## 7. Learning From Failure / Failure Logging

**What is it?**
Failure logging means writing down what didn't work, why it didn't work, and what you learned from it — treating failed tests as useful information, not something to hide.

**Why does it matter?**
Innovation involves a lot of things not working the first time. If you don't record what failed and why, you risk repeating the same mistake instead of learning from it.

**Example**
Your first hallway-alert design was ignored by most students. Logging that it was too small and placed too low taught you exactly what to change.

**Common mistake**
Only recording tests that "worked" and quietly leaving out the ones that didn't.

**Try this**
After your next test, write down one thing that didn't work and your best guess at why.

**Key takeaway**
A logged failure is progress. An unrecorded one is a repeated mistake.

**Think about it**
What's something that didn't work in your project that you haven't written down yet?

**Use this in your project**
Add every failed or partial test to your Iteration Log, including what you think caused it.

## 8. Feedback → Revision

**What is it?**
This is the practice of taking what you learned from testing and actually changing your prototype in response — rather than collecting feedback and leaving the design the same.

**Why does it matter?**
Feedback only matters if it leads to a real change. Collecting feedback and ignoring it defeats the purpose of testing in the first place.

**Example**
After learning students didn't notice the hallway alert, you move it to eye level and make it brighter — a direct response to what you observed.

**Common mistake**
Gathering feedback, agreeing it's valid, and then building the next version exactly the same way anyway.

**Try this**
For one piece of feedback you received, write down exactly what you're changing in response to it.

**Key takeaway**
Feedback that doesn't change anything wasn't really used.

**Think about it**
Is there feedback you've received that you haven't actually acted on yet?

**Use this in your project**
Connect each entry in your Iteration Log to a specific change you made because of it.

---

## 9. Versioning / Iteration History

**What is it?**
Versioning means keeping track of each distinct version of your prototype over time — what changed between them, and why — instead of only keeping the most recent version.

**Why does it matter?**
Your iteration history is evidence of your learning process. It shows reviewers (and you) how your thinking evolved, not just what you ended up with.

**Example**
V1: paper sketch, unclear icon. V2: bigger icon, still missed by some students. V3: added color and sound, most students noticed it correctly.

**Common mistake**
Only keeping the final version and losing track of what earlier versions looked like or why they changed.

**Try this**
Label your current prototype version, and briefly describe what changed from the previous one.

**Key takeaway**
Your version history tells the story of what you learned.

**Think about it**
If someone only saw your final version, would they understand how you got there?

**Use this in your project**
Keep a running V1 → V2 → V3 record in your Iteration Log.

## 10. Team Execution & Task Tracking

**What is it?**
This means actually tracking who is doing what, by when, and what's blocking progress — using a simple system like a task list or Kanban board, instead of relying on memory or informal conversations.

**Why does it matter?**
As testing and revisions pile up, it's easy to lose track of what's been done, what's next, and who's responsible. A simple tracking system keeps the team moving without confusion.

**Example**
A simple three-column list: To Do, In Progress, Done — with each task assigned to a specific team member and a rough deadline.

**Common mistake**
Assuming everyone remembers what they're supposed to be doing without writing it down anywhere.

**Try this**
List your team's current tasks with an owner and a deadline for each.

**Key takeaway**
If it's not written down, it's easy to lose track of.

**Think about it**
Right now, could every member of your team say exactly what they're responsible for this week?

**Use this in your project**
Keep an active task list alongside your Iteration Log.

## 11. Formal Experiment Controls *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
This is a more rigorous version of experiment design — deliberately identifying your independent variable (what you change), dependent variable (what you measure), controlled conditions (what you try to keep consistent), and, when appropriate, a comparison or control group.

**Why does it matter?**
As projects get more complex, informal testing isn't precise enough to draw a reliable conclusion. Formal controls let you be confident about what actually caused your result. Not every innovation test needs a control group — usability, product, process, and engineering projects often don't — but every test benefits from being clear about what you changed, measured, and held constant.

**Example**
Testing two hallway sign designs with similar groups of students, keeping the time of day and hallway location the same, and measuring one specific outcome: how many students correctly used the new route.

**Common mistake**
Comparing results between groups that differ in more ways than just the thing being tested.

**Try this**
For your next test, name your independent variable, dependent variable, controlled conditions, and (if relevant) a comparison group.

**Key takeaway**
Rigorous testing means knowing exactly what you changed, what you measured, and what stayed the same.

**Think about it**
Could someone else look at your test setup and understand exactly what was being compared?

**Use this in your project**
Add your independent/dependent variables, controlled conditions, and comparison group (if used) to your Test Plan.

---

## 12. Git & Technical Documentation *(HS CORE, when relevant)*

**What is it?**
For software-based projects, this means using version control (like Git) to track code changes over time, and writing clear documentation that explains how the project works.

**Why does it matter?**
As code changes, it's easy to lose track of what changed and why — or to lose working code entirely. Version control and documentation protect your work and make it understandable to others.

**Example**
Using Git to save a working version of your code before trying a risky change, so you can go back if the new approach doesn't work.

**Common mistake**
Making major code changes without saving previous working versions, and losing progress when something breaks.

**Try this**
If your project involves code, set up a basic Git repository and commit your current working version before your next major change.

**Key takeaway**
Protect your work, and explain it clearly enough that someone else could understand it.

**Think about it**
If you lost your current files right now, would you be able to recover your progress?

**Use this in your project**
Link your code repository and a short technical README in your Iteration Log.

## Closing Reflection for Stage 4
**Can we build and test the critical assumption?**

Before moving to Stage 5 (IMPACT), a student should be able to show a real test of their MVP against a stated test question and success criteria, both observed and reported feedback, at least one meaningful learning from testing — including any failed, partial, surprising, or inconclusive result — and a version history showing at least one real revision based on evidence.
