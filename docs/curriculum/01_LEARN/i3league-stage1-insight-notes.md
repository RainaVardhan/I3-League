# i3League — Stage 1: INSIGHT
### Guiding Question: Whose problem is this, and how do I know?

---

## 1. Observation Skills

**What is it?**
Observation means paying attention to what is happening around you and noticing moments where people struggle, waste time, get confused, or create workarounds.

**Why does it matter?**
Many strong innovations begin with noticing something other people overlook. Before solving a problem, you first have to see it clearly.

**Example**
You notice that students regularly arrive late to class because one hallway becomes extremely crowded between periods.

**Common mistake**
Immediately deciding, "We need an app." That is already jumping to a solution.

**Try this**
For three days, write down five moments where you notice frustration, delay, confusion, waste, or inconvenience.

**Key takeaway**
Great innovators notice before they solve.

**Think about it**
What is something people around you have learned to tolerate that might actually be a problem worth solving?

**Use this in your project**
Add your strongest observations to your Stage 1 Evidence Notes.

## 2. Empathy Without Assumption

**What is it?**
Empathy means trying to understand what someone else is actually experiencing — their needs, frustrations, and constraints — without deciding for them what the problem or solution should be.

**Why does it matter?**
It's easy to assume you already know what someone needs. But if you guess wrong, you end up solving a problem nobody actually has.

**Example**
You assume younger students want a faster way to sign up for clubs. But when you actually ask them, they say the real problem is not knowing which clubs exist in the first place.

**Common mistake**
Deciding what a group needs based on your own experience instead of theirs.

**Try this**
Pick one person affected by a problem you're curious about. Ask them to describe it in their own words, and write down only what they say — not what you think they mean.

**Key takeaway**
Understand the problem through their eyes, not your assumptions.

**Think about it**
Have you ever assumed you knew what someone needed, and found out later you were wrong?

**Use this in your project**
Record one thing you learned from another person that changed or challenged your assumption.

## 3. Problem Scope

**What is it?**
Problem scope means figuring out how big or small a problem is — broad enough to matter, but narrow enough that you could realistically do something about it.

**Why does it matter?**
A problem that's too broad ("improve education") is impossible to act on. A problem that's too narrow ("my locker sticks") may not be worth a full project. Good projects sit in the middle: real, specific, and solvable.

**Example**
"Students are stressed" is too broad. "Students miss the morning bus because the sign-in line at the front office is too slow" is scoped well enough to actually work on.

**Common mistake**
Picking a problem so huge that no single team could meaningfully address it in one program cycle.

**Try this**
Take a broad problem you care about and narrow it down by asking: Who exactly? Where exactly? When exactly?

**Key takeaway**
The right problem is specific enough to solve, but real enough to matter.

**Think about it**
Is the problem you're considering something you could realistically make progress on this year?

**Use this in your project**
Rewrite your problem until you can clearly name who, where, and what.

## 4. Ethical Interviewing & Consent

**What is it?**
Ethical interviewing means talking to people about a problem in a way that respects their time, privacy, and right to say no — and always being honest about why you're asking.

**Why does it matter?**
Good research depends on people trusting you enough to be honest. If you pressure people, trick them, or use their words without permission, your research becomes unreliable — and unfair to them.

**Example**
Before interviewing a classmate about a problem, you explain who you are, why you're asking, and that they can skip any question or stop at any time.

**Common mistake**
Asking leading questions that push someone toward the answer you already want, or using someone's story without asking if that's okay.

**Try this**
Write a one-sentence introduction you'd say before starting any interview, explaining what the interview is for and that participation is optional.

**Key takeaway**
People trust you with their story — treat it that way.

**Think about it**
How would you feel if someone used something you said without telling you why or asking first?

**Use this in your project**
Use your interview introduction before collecting firsthand evidence.

## 5. Team Charter

**What is it?**
A Team Charter is a short, simple agreement your team makes at the start of a project: who's doing what, how you'll communicate, and how you'll make decisions together.

**Why does it matter?**
Teams that skip this step often run into avoidable conflict later — confusion over who's responsible for what, or disagreements with no clear way to resolve them.

**Example**
A team agrees: "We'll check in every Tuesday. If we disagree on a decision, we'll vote. If it's a tie, our coach breaks it."

**Common mistake**
Assuming everyone already agrees on roles and decision-making without ever actually saying it out loud.

**Try this**
With your team, write down: each person's role, how often you'll check in, and what happens when you disagree.

**Key takeaway**
A few minutes of agreement now saves hours of conflict later.

**Think about it**
What's one disagreement that could come up on your team, and how would you want to handle it?

**Use this in your project**
Save your team agreement as part of your project record.

## Closing Reflection for Stage 1
**Whose problem is this, and how do I know?**

Before moving to Stage 2 (INVESTIGATE), a student should be able to answer this question in their own words, backed by at least one piece of firsthand evidence.
