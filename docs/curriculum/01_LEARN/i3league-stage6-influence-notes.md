# i3League — Stage 6: INFLUENCE
### Guiding Question: Can we defend it, sustain it, and convince someone to take the next step?

### Mental Model: Who needs to care? → What do they need to know? → What does the evidence prove? → Can we show it? → Can we defend it? → What would implementation require? → What should happen next?

Not every innovation needs customers. Not every innovation needs revenue. Not every innovation needs investors. But every innovation needs a credible path to implementation, adoption, continuation, or further investigation.

---

## 1. Know Your Audience / Audience Adaptation

**What is it?**
This means understanding who you're presenting to, and adjusting what you emphasize based on what that specific audience needs to understand or decide.

**Why does it matter?**
A user, a school administrator, an engineer, and a judge all care about different things. Presenting the same way to everyone means you'll miss what actually matters to each of them.

**Example**
To a school administrator, you'd emphasize feasibility and cost. To a potential user, you'd emphasize how it makes their day easier. To a judge, you'd emphasize evidence and rigor.

**Common mistake**
Giving the exact same pitch to every audience, regardless of what they actually care about.

**Try this**
Before your next pitch, write down what this specific audience needs to walk away understanding or deciding.

**Key takeaway**
Speak to what this audience needs to know, not just what you want to say.

**Think about it**
If two different audiences heard your current pitch, would it actually land the same way for both?

**Use this in your project**
Note your target audience and their key concern at the top of your Pitch Planner.

---

## 2. Evidence-Based Storytelling

**What is it?**
This means turning your entire innovation journey — problem, research, concept, testing, results — into a clear, honest narrative, without exaggerating or turning it into hype.

**Why does it matter?**
A pitch that's just a list of facts is hard to follow. A pitch that's all hype isn't credible. The strongest pitches tell a true story that's also backed by real evidence at every step.

**Example**
"We noticed students were late because of one crowded hallway. We confirmed it with data. We tested three ideas and picked the strongest one. Here's what we built, what we learned, and what changed."

**Common mistake**
Either listing facts with no narrative thread, or telling an exciting story that oversells what actually happened.

**Try this**
Tell your project's story in under two minutes, hitting one key piece of evidence at each stage.

**Key takeaway**
A good story is still true at every step.

**Think about it**
Does your current pitch feel like a real journey, or just a list of things you did?

**Use this in your project**
Draft your two-minute story arc in your Pitch Planner.

---

## 3. Visual Communication & Data Visualization

**What is it?**
This means showing your evidence clearly — through charts, diagrams, photos, prototypes, or before/after comparisons — instead of only describing it in paragraphs.

**Why does it matter?**
People understand a clear chart or image faster than a paragraph of numbers. Even a simple visual can make your evidence far easier to grasp and remember.

**Example**
A simple bar chart showing late arrivals before and after your solution communicates the result faster than a paragraph explaining the same numbers.

**Common mistake**
Putting dense paragraphs of text or raw numbers on a slide instead of a simple visual.

**Try this**
Turn your single most important piece of evidence into one simple chart, diagram, or before/after image.

**Key takeaway**
Show it — don't just describe it.

**Think about it**
What's the one result in your project that would be clearest as a picture instead of a sentence?

**Use this in your project**
Include at least one visual representation of your evidence in your final Pitch.

---

## 4. Demo Skills

**What is it?**
Demo skills mean showing your actual solution in action — letting people see or try it — rather than only talking about what it does.

**Why does it matter?**
Seeing something work is more convincing than hearing it described. A demo also shows confidence that your solution is real, not just an idea on paper.

**Example**
Instead of describing how the hallway alert looks, you show the actual prototype and walk through exactly what a student would see and do.

**Common mistake**
Over-explaining the demo instead of letting people actually see it work.

**Try this**
Practice demoing your prototype in under 60 seconds, saying as little as possible while it does the talking.

**Key takeaway**
Let people see it, don't just hear about it.

**Think about it**
Could someone understand your solution just by watching the demo, with minimal explanation from you?

**Use this in your project**
Plan your demo sequence and timing in your Pitch Planner.

## 5. Defending Your Evidence

**What is it?**
This means being ready to clearly explain where your claims came from — what was tested, what changed, and what still remains uncertain — if someone asks you to back them up.

**Why does it matter?**
A confident claim isn't the same as a defensible one. Reviewers and decision-makers will often ask "how do you know that?" — you need a real answer, not just confidence.

**Example**
If you claim your solution reduced late arrivals, you should be able to say exactly how you measured that, over what time period, and what your baseline was.

**Common mistake**
Stating a result confidently but being unable to explain exactly how you know it's true.

**Try this**
For your top three claims, write down exactly what evidence backs each one up.

**Key takeaway**
Be ready to show your work, not just your conclusion.

**Think about it**
If someone asked "how do you know that?" about your biggest claim, what would you say?

**Use this in your project**
Link each major claim in your Pitch to the evidence behind it in your Portfolio.

## 6. Q&A, Objections & Critical Questions

**What is it?**
This means handling questions and pushback well — actually listening to what was asked, answering it honestly, admitting what you don't know, and defending your decisions with evidence rather than defensiveness.

**Why does it matter?**
Tough questions are a normal part of real-world innovation. How you handle them says a lot about how well you actually understand your own project.

**Example**
If asked "what if this doesn't scale to the whole school?", a strong answer acknowledges the limitation honestly and explains what you'd want to test next, rather than pretending it's not a concern.

**Common mistake**
Getting defensive, dodging the question, or pretending to know something you don't.

**Try this**
List the three toughest questions someone could ask about your project, and practice answering them honestly.

**Key takeaway**
Answer what was actually asked, and it's okay to say what you don't know yet.

**Think about it**
What's the question you're most nervous about being asked?

**Use this in your project**
Prepare answers to your toughest anticipated questions in your Pitch Planner.

---

## 7. Implementation & Sustainability Model

**What is it?**
This means explaining what it would actually take for your solution to keep running beyond the i3League program — who uses it, who maintains it, and what it needs to survive long-term.

**Why does it matter?**
A great pilot that can't continue afterward has limited real-world value. Thinking this through shows you're considering your idea as something real, not just a project for a grade or competition.

**Example**
Explaining who would maintain the hallway-alert signage after the program ends, and how the school would keep it updated.

**Common mistake**
Presenting a working pilot without any plan for what happens to it afterward.

**Try this**
Answer: who uses it, who benefits, who decides, who maintains it, and what makes it sustainable?

**Key takeaway**
A real solution needs a real plan to keep existing.

**Think about it**
If your program ended tomorrow, would your solution keep running?

**Use this in your project**
Include your implementation and sustainability plan in your Pitch and Portfolio.

## 8. Clear Ask / Next Step

**What is it?**
The Ask is a specific, concrete thing you want from your audience — an approval, a resource, a mentor, a pilot opportunity — stated clearly at the end of your pitch.

**Why does it matter?**
The goal of a pitch is not applause. The goal is a decision. Ending vaguely leaves your audience unsure what you actually want them to do next.

**Example**
"We need approval to pilot this with 50 students," or "We're looking for a mentor with engineering experience," or "We need $500 to build the next prototype."

**Common mistake**
Ending with "Thank you for listening" instead of a specific request or recommended next step.

**Try this**
Write your Ask in one sentence: "Based on what we learned, here is the next step we recommend, and here is what we need to make it happen."

**Key takeaway**
End with a decision, not just a thank-you.

**Think about it**
If your audience wanted to say yes right now, would they know exactly what they were saying yes to?

**Use this in your project**
Write your final Ask as the closing line of your Pitch.

---

## 9. Portfolio Documentation

**What is it?**
This means assembling the evidence and work from all six stages — problem, research, concepts, prototypes, results, and pitch — into one complete Innovation Portfolio.

**Why does it matter?**
Your portfolio is the full record of your learning and evidence, not just your final pitch. It shows the complete journey, which is often more convincing than the final result alone.

**Example**
A portfolio that includes your Validated Problem Statement, Research Brief, Concept Portfolio, Prototype and Iteration Log, Impact Report, and final Pitch — in one place.

**Common mistake**
Only presenting the final polished result, with no record of the process that led to it.

**Try this**
Check that every required artifact from Stages 1–5 is included and organized in your Portfolio.

**Key takeaway**
The portfolio is your proof of the whole journey, not just the ending.

**Think about it**
If a reviewer only had your portfolio and no chance to ask you questions, would they understand your full journey?

**Use this in your project**
Assemble your complete Innovation Portfolio before your final pitch.

## 10. Reflection & Future Direction

**What is it?**
This means honestly looking back at what you believed at the start, what you learned along the way, and what changed in your thinking — plus what you'd test or improve next.

**Why does it matter?**
Innovation doesn't end with one pitch. Reflecting on your own growth, and naming what you'd do next, shows real ownership of the project rather than treating it as finished and forgotten.

**Example**
"We started out assuming students just needed reminders. We learned the real issue was hallway layout — that changed our whole approach. Next, we'd want to test this across more hallways."

**Common mistake**
Skipping reflection entirely, or only reflecting on what went well without naming what you'd change.

**Try this**
Answer the three reflection questions: What did I believe before? What did I learn? What changed because of what I learned?

**Key takeaway**
The project isn't just what you built — it's what you learned.

**Think about it**
What would you do differently if you started this project again today?

**Use this in your project**
Include your reflection and future direction as the closing section of your Portfolio.

## 11. Budgeting & Resource Planning *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
This means identifying what money, people, technology, materials, time, or permissions your solution would actually need to be implemented for real.

**Why does it matter?**
An idea without a realistic resource plan is hard to actually put into action. Thinking this through shows decision-makers you understand what implementation genuinely requires.

**Example**
Listing the cost of signage materials, the time needed from a facilities staff member, and the approval required from school administration.

**Common mistake**
Presenting an idea as ready to implement without naming what resources it would actually require.

**Try this**
List every resource — money, people, time, materials, permissions — your solution would need to actually be implemented.

**Key takeaway**
A real plan names what it actually needs.

**Think about it**
Is there a resource your plan depends on that you haven't clearly identified yet?

**Use this in your project**
Include a resource list in your Implementation & Sustainability Model.

## 12. Revenue / Funding / Support Model *(HS CORE, when relevant)*

**What is it?**
This means identifying how your solution would be financially supported long-term — which could mean revenue, grants, sponsorship, school funding, nonprofit support, or institutional budget, depending on the type of project.

**Why does it matter?**
Not every project needs a business model, but every project that continues past a pilot needs some way of being funded or supported. Naming this honestly shows realistic thinking about sustainability.

**Example**
A school-based solution might rely on existing school budget rather than needing new revenue. A community nonprofit project might require a small grant.

**Common mistake**
Assuming every project needs a subscription or sales-based revenue model, even when that doesn't fit the type of solution.

**Try this**
Identify which funding or support model actually fits your type of project — it's okay if the answer is "existing school resources."

**Key takeaway**
Match your funding model to what your project actually is.

**Think about it**
Does your solution genuinely need new funding, or could it run on resources that already exist?

**Use this in your project**
Note your funding or support model in your Implementation & Sustainability Model.

---

## 13. Partnership Strategy *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
This means identifying who else — people, departments, organizations — would need to be involved for your solution to actually work in the real world.

**Why does it matter?**
Very few solutions succeed in isolation. Naming the partners you'd need shows you've thought realistically about what implementation actually requires.

**Example**
A hallway-alert solution might require partnership with the school's facilities team and administration to install and maintain signage.

**Common mistake**
Assuming your team could implement everything alone, without support from anyone else.

**Try this**
List who would need to say "yes" or get involved for your solution to actually happen.

**Key takeaway**
Real implementation usually needs real partners.

**Think about it**
Who haven't you talked to yet who would need to be involved for this to actually happen?

**Use this in your project**
List your key partners in your Implementation & Sustainability Model.

---

## 14. Risk & Mitigation *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
This means identifying what could prevent your solution from being implemented successfully, and having a plan for reducing that risk.

**Why does it matter?**
Naming implementation risks honestly — rather than pretending everything will go smoothly — makes your plan more credible and shows you've thought it through.

**Example**
Risk: students might ignore the alert after the novelty wears off. Mitigation: periodically refresh the design or add a simple reminder.

**Common mistake**
Presenting an implementation plan with no acknowledgment of what could go wrong.

**Try this**
Name your top implementation risk and one specific way you'd reduce it.

**Key takeaway**
A credible plan names its risks and how to handle them.

**Think about it**
What's the most likely reason your implementation plan could fail?

**Use this in your project**
Add a risk and mitigation note to your Implementation & Sustainability Model.

## 15. Executive Summary *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
An executive summary is a short, one-page version of your entire project — problem, evidence, solution, results, and ask — written so a busy decision-maker can understand it quickly.

**Why does it matter?**
Not everyone who needs to make a decision about your project will sit through your full pitch. A strong executive summary lets them get the essential picture fast.

**Example**
A one-page document covering: the problem, what you found, what you built, what changed, and what you're asking for — each in a sentence or two.

**Common mistake**
Writing an executive summary that's really just a shortened story, without including the actual ask or key evidence.

**Try this**
Write your entire project in one page, covering problem, evidence, solution, result, and ask.

**Key takeaway**
A busy decision-maker should be able to understand your project from one page.

**Think about it**
If someone only read your executive summary, would they understand what you're asking them to do?

**Use this in your project**
Include a one-page executive summary in your final Portfolio.

## 16. Go-to-Market / Adoption Strategy *(STRETCH, when relevant)*

**What is it?**
This means planning how your solution would actually reach the people or organizations that should use or implement it, beyond your initial pilot group.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution that works well in a small pilot still needs a realistic path to reach a wider audience if it's meant to grow beyond the original test group.

**Example**
Planning how a hallway-alert solution that worked in one hallway could be introduced to the rest of the school, or to other schools in the district.

**Common mistake**
Assuming a solution will spread on its own without any real plan for reaching new users or adopters.

**Try this**
Sketch one realistic path for how your solution could reach beyond its initial pilot group.

**Key takeaway**
Growth needs a plan, not just hope.

**Think about it**
If your pilot succeeded, what would the very next step to reach more people actually look like?

**Use this in your project**
Include a go-to-market or adoption plan in your Portfolio if your project is ready for this level of planning.

## 17. Formal Market / Opportunity Sizing *(STRETCH, when relevant)*

**What is it?**
This means estimating, with real data, how large the population, market, or opportunity is for your solution — only appropriate for projects where a meaningful estimate is actually possible.

**Why does it matter?**
For certain advanced projects, showing the scale of the opportunity can strengthen your case for further investment or expansion — but only if the estimate is grounded in real data, not guesswork.

**Example**
Estimating how many schools in your district face a similar hallway-congestion problem, based on real enrollment and building data.

**Common mistake**
Presenting an invented or unsupported "market size" number to sound more impressive.

**Try this**
If attempting this, identify a real data source you could use to estimate the size of your opportunity.

**Key takeaway**
An opportunity estimate is only as credible as the data behind it.

**Think about it**
Do you actually have data to support an opportunity estimate, or would it be a guess dressed up as a number?

**Use this in your project**
Include this only if your project has real data to support a credible estimate.

---

## 18. Technical Architecture / Deployment Plan *(STRETCH, when relevant)*

**What is it?**
For technically complex projects, this means explaining how a real implementation would actually operate — the systems, tools, and technical steps involved.

**Why does it matter?**
For projects with real technical complexity, explaining how it would actually be built and deployed shows depth beyond the prototype stage, which matters for advanced technical review.

**Example**
Explaining what platform a hallway-sensor system would run on, how data would be collected and stored, and what would be needed to install it school-wide.

**Common mistake**
Skipping technical detail entirely for a project that's advanced enough to need it, leaving reviewers unsure if it's actually feasible.

**Try this**
If your project is technically complex, sketch the basic technical components a real deployment would require.

**Key takeaway**
Technical credibility comes from explaining how it would actually work, not just that it does.

**Think about it**
If an engineer asked how your solution would actually be deployed, could you answer beyond "it just would"?

**Use this in your project**
Include this only for projects with genuine technical complexity that requires it.

---

## Closing Reflection for Stage 6
**Can we defend it, sustain it, and convince someone to take the next step?**

The final Pitch and Portfolio should show: a story backed by evidence at every stage, at least one clear visual and one live demo, honest answers to hard questions, a realistic implementation and sustainability plan, and a specific closing Ask. The goal of the pitch is not applause — it's a decision.
