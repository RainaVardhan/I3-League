# i3League — Stage 5: IMPACT
### Mental Model: What changed? → Compared to what? → How do we know? → Who benefited? → At what cost? → What didn't we prove? → Would it still work at scale?

---

## 1. What Does Impact Mean?

**What is it?**
Impact means the actual, measurable difference your solution made — not how clever it sounds, or how hard your team worked on it.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution can be interesting and still not actually help anyone. IMPACT is where you find out whether your idea made a real difference, based on evidence rather than effort or enthusiasm.

**Example**
Building a hallway-alert prototype is effort. Showing that late arrivals to class actually dropped after students used it is impact.

**Common mistake**
Confusing "we built something" or "people liked it" with "it actually made a difference."

**Try this**
Finish this sentence: "Because of our solution, ___ changed for ___."

**Key takeaway**
Impact is a measured difference, not a finished project.

**Think about it**
If you had to prove your project made a difference — not describe it, prove it — what would you show?

**Use this in your project**
Write your impact statement at the top of your Impact Report.

---

## 2. Choosing the Right Success Metric

**What is it?**
A success metric is the specific, measurable thing you'll track to know whether your solution is working — chosen because it actually reflects the problem you set out to solve.

**Why does it matter?**
The wrong metric can make a weak solution look strong, or a strong solution look weak. Picking a metric that's easy to measure but doesn't matter defeats the purpose of measuring at all.

**Example**
For the hallway-alert idea, "number of students who downloaded the alert" is easy to measure but doesn't prove much. "Number of late arrivals to class" is harder to measure but actually reflects the problem.

**Common mistake**
Choosing a metric because it's easy to collect, not because it actually reflects success.

**Try this**
Ask: if this metric went up, would it actually mean the real problem got better?

**Key takeaway**
Measure what matters, not just what's easy to count.

**Think about it**
Is your current metric measuring the real problem, or something related but different?

**Use this in your project**
Name your chosen success metric and explain why it reflects the real problem in your Impact Report.

---

## 3. Baseline / Benchmark / Comparison vs. Result

**What is it?**
A meaningful reference point is what you compare your result against to know if anything actually changed. Depending on your project, this may be a before-state baseline, a benchmark, a control or comparison condition, an existing solution or standard, or another credible reference point appropriate to what you're testing. The result is what things look like after. Without a reference point, you can't really know if anything improved.

**Why does it matter?**
This is the heart of IMPACT. Without a credible comparison, a student may measure something but not actually know whether they improved it, since there's nothing to compare the result to.

**Example**
Baseline: 15 late arrivals per week before the hallway alert. Result: 6 late arrivals per week after using it. (A different kind of project might instead compare against a benchmark — e.g., an existing traffic-flow standard — or a control condition that didn't receive the intervention.)

**Common mistake**
Measuring only the "after" result and never establishing what things looked like before, or what a credible comparison point would be.

**Try this**
Write down your baseline, benchmark, or comparison condition before you test your solution — not after.

**Key takeaway**
You can't know if something improved without knowing what you're comparing it to.

**Think about it**
Do you actually have a real reference point, or are you estimating it after the fact?

**Use this in your project**
Report your reference point and result side by side in your Impact Report.

## 4. Quantitative vs. Qualitative Evidence

**What is it?**
Quantitative evidence is numbers — counts, percentages, measurements. Qualitative evidence is descriptive — quotes, observations, stories. Strong impact reporting often uses both.

**Why does it matter?**
Numbers can show that something changed. Stories and quotes can help explain why it changed, or what it felt like for the people affected. Relying on only one type gives an incomplete picture.

**Example**
Quantitative: late arrivals dropped from 15 to 6 per week. Qualitative: a student said, "I actually know which hallway to take now instead of guessing."

**Common mistake**
Reporting only a number, with no explanation of what it means to the people affected — or the reverse, only stories with no measurement at all.

**Try this**
Pair one number from your results with one real quote or observation that helps explain it.

**Key takeaway**
Numbers show what changed. Stories help explain why.

**Think about it**
Does your Impact Report currently lean too heavily on just one type of evidence?

**Use this in your project**
Include at least one quantitative and one qualitative piece of evidence in your Impact Report.

## 5. Data Analysis & Interpretation Basics

**What is it?**
This means looking carefully at your results and figuring out what they actually show — including checking for patterns, being honest about what's unclear, and not jumping to the first conclusion that supports your idea.

**Why does it matter?**
Raw results don't interpret themselves. Two people could look at the same data and draw different conclusions — your job is to interpret it carefully and honestly, not just in the way that makes your project look best.

**Example**
Late arrivals dropped, but so did overall attendance that week — meaning the drop might be partly explained by something else, not just your solution.

**Common mistake**
Picking the most flattering interpretation of the data without checking for other possible explanations.

**Try this**
Look at your results and ask: is there another reasonable explanation for this change, besides my solution?

**Key takeaway**
Data doesn't speak for itself — you have to interpret it honestly.

**Think about it**
Is there an alternative explanation for your results that you haven't fully considered?

**Use this in your project**
Include your interpretation of the data — not just the raw numbers — in your Impact Report.

---

## 6. Responsible Claims

**What is it?**
A responsible claim is a statement about your results that doesn't overstate what your evidence actually supports.

**Why does it matter?**
It's tempting to describe your results in the most impressive way possible. But if your claim goes further than your evidence supports, your report becomes misleading — even if you didn't mean it to be.

**Example**
Overstated: "Our solution eliminates hallway congestion." Responsible: "In our two-week pilot with one hallway, late arrivals dropped by 60%."

**Common mistake**
Turning a small, specific result into a big, general claim it doesn't actually support.

**Try this**
Read your impact claim out loud and ask: does my evidence actually support this exact statement?

**Key takeaway**
Say only what your evidence can back up.

**Think about it**
Is there a claim in your report that's bigger than what your data actually shows?

**Use this in your project**
Rewrite any overstated claims in your Impact Report so they match your actual evidence.

## 7. Limitations & Research Integrity

**What is it?**
Limitations are the honest boundaries of what your results actually prove — small sample size, short time frame, a specific location, or anything else that means your results might not apply everywhere.

**Why does it matter?**
Every project has limitations. Naming them honestly makes your report more credible, not less — it shows you understand exactly what your evidence does and doesn't prove.

**Example**
"This result is based on one hallway over two weeks. It may not reflect what would happen across the whole school or over a full semester."

**Common mistake**
Leaving limitations out of the report entirely, hoping no one asks.

**Try this**
Write down the one limitation that, if pointed out by a reviewer, you'd have the hardest time defending against — and include it anyway.

**Key takeaway**
Naming your limitations builds trust, it doesn't weaken your project.

**Think about it**
What's the first question a skeptical reviewer would ask about your results?

**Use this in your project**
Include a clearly labeled Limitations section in your Impact Report.

---

## 8. Unintended Consequences

**What is it?**
Unintended consequences are effects your solution had that you didn't plan for or expect — which can be neutral, helpful, or harmful to someone.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution can "work" for its main goal and still create a new problem somewhere else. Innovators are responsible for looking beyond just the result they were hoping for.

**Example**
The hallway alert reduced late arrivals, but also caused more crowding in a different hallway as students changed their routes.

**Common mistake**
Only checking whether the intended goal improved, without checking for side effects elsewhere.

**Try this**
Ask: did anything change — for better or worse — that we weren't specifically trying to measure?

**Key takeaway**
Check for effects you weren't looking for, not just the one you were.

**Think about it**
Is there a group or area that might have been affected by your solution in a way you haven't checked?

**Use this in your project**
Note any unintended consequences you observed in your Impact Report.

---

## 9. Impact Across Stakeholders

**What is it?**
This means checking whether your solution's effect was the same for everyone it touched, or whether it helped some people while affecting others differently.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution can look successful overall while actually helping one group and creating a new burden for another. Breaking down impact by stakeholder group gives a fuller, fairer picture.

**Example**
The hallway alert helped most students, but made things harder for a student using a wheelchair if the suggested faster route wasn't accessible.

**Common mistake**
Reporting only an overall average result without checking whether it held true across different groups.

**Try this**
List the different groups affected by your solution and note whether the impact was the same for each.

**Key takeaway**
An average result can hide very different experiences underneath it.

**Think about it**
Is there a group affected by your solution that you haven't specifically checked in on?

**Use this in your project**
Break down your impact by relevant stakeholder group in your Impact Report where possible.

## 10. Cost vs. Benefit

**What is it?**
This means weighing what your solution costs — time, money, effort, resources — against the actual benefit it produced, to judge whether it was worth it.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution that produces a small benefit at a very high cost may not be worth continuing, even if it technically "worked." Thinking this through helps you judge your project realistically.

**Example**
A solution that costs very little time and no money to maintain, but reduces late arrivals significantly, is a strong cost-benefit trade. One that requires expensive new hardware for a small improvement is weaker.

**Common mistake**
Reporting only the benefit and ignoring what it actually cost to achieve it.

**Try this**
List what your solution cost to build and run, next to the benefit it produced.

**Key takeaway**
A real benefit still has to be worth its cost.

**Think about it**
If you had to justify your project's cost to someone controlling the budget, could you?

**Use this in your project**
Add a simple cost-benefit summary to your Impact Report.

## 11. Correlation vs. Causation *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
Correlation means two things happened around the same time or seem related. Causation means one thing actually caused the other. Just because two things are related doesn't mean one caused the other.

**Why does it matter?**
It's easy to assume your solution caused an improvement, when something else happening at the same time might actually be responsible.

**Example**
Late arrivals dropped the same week your hallway alert launched — but that was also the first cold week of the year, when fewer students lingered outside before class.

**Common mistake**
Assuming any change that happened after your solution launched must have been caused by it.

**Try this**
List anything else that changed around the same time as your result, and consider whether it could explain part of the change.

**Key takeaway**
Related in time doesn't automatically mean caused by.

**Think about it**
What else was happening at the same time as your result that could offer a different explanation?

**Use this in your project**
Address possible alternative explanations for your result in your Impact Report.

## 12. Leading vs. Lagging Indicators *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
A leading indicator is an early signal that predicts a future result. A lagging indicator confirms a result that has already happened. Both are useful, but they answer different questions.

**Why does it matter?**
If you only track lagging indicators, you might not notice a problem — or a success — until much later. Leading indicators can help you course-correct sooner.

**Example**
Leading indicator: how many students say they understand the hallway alert. Lagging indicator: the actual number of late arrivals weeks later.

**Common mistake**
Waiting only for the final, lagging result instead of tracking earlier signals along the way.

**Try this**
Identify one early signal you could track now that might predict your final result later.

**Key takeaway**
Early signals can tell you something before the final result is in.

**Think about it**
Is there a leading indicator you could be tracking that you're currently ignoring?

**Use this in your project**
Note at least one leading and one lagging indicator in your Impact Report.

---

## 13. Adoption, Uptake & Usage Metrics *(HS CORE, when relevant)*

**What is it?**
This tracks whether people are actually choosing your solution and sticking with it — or, for non-user-facing projects, whether it's being adopted, implemented, followed, or put into practice. Depending on the project, this could mean usage, uptake, participation, compliance, or utilization.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution can work perfectly in testing and still fail in the real world if it isn't actually adopted or sustained — whether that means people using an app, a school adopting a policy, or a process being followed consistently.

**Example**
Tracking what percentage of students who saw the hallway alert actually changed their route because of it, and whether they kept using it after the first week. For a policy-style project, this might instead be the percentage of classrooms actually following a new procedure.

**Common mistake**
Reporting that a solution "works" based only on a single test, without checking whether it was adopted or sustained afterward.

**Try this**
If possible, track whether people who tried your solution kept using, following, or implementing it without being prompted.

**Key takeaway**
Working once and being adopted long-term are two different things.

**Think about it**
Do you have any evidence about whether your solution would keep being used or followed without being enforced?

**Use this in your project**
Include an adoption, uptake, or usage metric in your Impact Report if your project allows for it.

---

## 14. Scalability *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
Scalability means checking whether your solution would still work if it were used by many more people, in many more places, than your original test.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution that works well for a small pilot group might break down, become too expensive, or lose effectiveness at a much larger scale.

**Example**
A hallway alert system tested in one hallway might work differently — better or worse — if it had to cover every hallway in the school at once.

**Common mistake**
Assuming that because a solution worked in a small test, it would automatically work the same way at full scale.

**Try this**
Ask: if this were used 10 times bigger than our test, what might break or need to change?

**Key takeaway**
What works small doesn't automatically work big.

**Think about it**
What's the first thing that would likely break if your solution scaled up significantly?

**Use this in your project**
Add a short scalability consideration to your Impact Report.

## 15. Cost per User / Cost per Use / Pilot Cost

**What is it?**
This means breaking down the total cost of your solution into a simple per-person or per-use number — a manageable way to understand cost before using more advanced financial terms.

**Why does it matter?**
A total cost number can be hard to judge on its own. Breaking it into cost per user or per use makes it easier to compare against the benefit it provides.

**Example**
If your pilot cost $50 total and served 25 students, that's $2 per student — a concrete, comparable number.

**Common mistake**
Only reporting a total cost without breaking it down into a per-person or per-use figure that's easier to evaluate.

**Try this**
Take your total pilot cost and divide it by the number of people who used or benefited from it.

**Key takeaway**
A cost is easier to judge once it's broken down per person or per use.

**Think about it**
Would your project still seem worth it if you had to explain the cost per student out loud?

**Use this in your project**
Include a cost-per-user or cost-per-use figure in your Impact Report.

## 16. Unit Economics & ROI *(HS CORE, where applicable)*

**What is it?**
Unit economics is the formal version of cost-per-user thinking — understanding the cost and value associated with one "unit" (one user, one transaction) at scale. ROI (Return on Investment) compares the benefit gained to the cost required to get it.

**Why does it matter?**
As projects grow more complex or move toward real implementation, understanding unit economics and ROI helps determine whether a solution is not just effective, but sustainable.

**Example**
If a solution costs $2 per student to run but saves the school an estimated $10 worth of lost instructional time per student, that's a strong ROI.

**Common mistake**
Treating a large total benefit as automatically good, without checking whether it's actually a strong return relative to what was invested.

**Try this**
Calculate a simple ROI for your project: (benefit − cost) ÷ cost.

**Key takeaway**
A good return means the benefit clearly outweighs what it cost to get there.

**Think about it**
If you had to defend your project's ROI to someone controlling a real budget, what would you say?

**Use this in your project**
Include a simple ROI calculation in your Impact Report where applicable.

## 17. Advanced Causal Analysis / Forecasting *(STRETCH)*

**What is it?**
This is a deeper level of impact analysis — using more rigorous methods to isolate cause and effect, or projecting how your results might play out over a longer time frame or larger scale.

**Why does it matter?**
For advanced or complex projects, basic correlation checks may not be enough. Stronger causal analysis and forecasting can make an impact claim much more credible to expert reviewers.

**Example**
Comparing your pilot hallway to a similar hallway that didn't get the alert, to more confidently isolate the alert's actual effect from other factors.

**Common mistake**
Using advanced-sounding language without the underlying rigor to actually support it.

**Try this**
If attempting this level of analysis, identify a comparison group or baseline period that wasn't exposed to your solution, to strengthen your causal claim.

**Key takeaway**
Advanced claims require advanced evidence to back them up.

**Think about it**
Does your project have access to a genuine comparison case, or would this analysis be more speculative than rigorous?

**Use this in your project**
Include this level of analysis only if your project has the data to support it credibly.

---

## Closing Reflection for Stage 5
**What changed? Compared to what? How do we know? Who benefited? At what cost? What didn't we prove? Would it still work at scale?**

Before moving to Stage 6 (INFLUENCE), a student should be able to show a clear baseline-to-result comparison, both quantitative and qualitative evidence, an honest account of limitations and any unintended consequences, and a stated cost relative to the benefit produced.
