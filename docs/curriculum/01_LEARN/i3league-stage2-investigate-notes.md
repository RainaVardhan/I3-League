# i3League — Stage 2: INVESTIGATE
### Guiding Principle: Good research can change your mind.

---

## 1. Basic Data Literacy

**What is it?**
Data literacy means being able to read and understand simple numbers and patterns — like sample size, percentages, averages, and outliers — well enough to know what they actually tell you.

**Why does it matter?**
Numbers can sound convincing even when they don't mean much. If you don't understand what's behind a statistic, you can be fooled by it — or accidentally mislead others with it.

**Example**
"90% of students said they'd use this" sounds strong — until you learn only 10 students were asked.

**Common mistake**
Treating a small or unrepresentative sample as if it proves something about everyone.

**Try this**
Find a statistic from your own research and ask: How many people was this based on? Who were they?

**Key takeaway**
A number is only as strong as the evidence behind it.

**Think about it**
Have you ever believed a statistic that later turned out to be less convincing once you knew the details?

**Use this in your project**
Note the sample size next to any statistic in your Research Brief.

## 2. Bias Awareness

**What is bias?**
Bias is when research is shaped — often without meaning to — by what you already believe, how you ask a question, or who you choose to ask.

**Why can it hurt research?**
If your research is biased, it can "prove" what you already think instead of showing you what's actually true. That means you might build a solution for a problem that doesn't really exist the way you think it does.

**Example of a leading survey question**
"Don't you think the cafeteria line is too slow?" — this pushes people toward agreeing with you.

**Better version**
"How would you describe your experience with the cafeteria line?"

**Common mistake**
Only asking people you expect to agree with you, or wording questions so there's really only one "right" answer.

**Try this**
Take one question from your own survey or interview and rewrite it to remove any hint of the answer you're hoping for.

**Key takeaway**
Good research should be willing to prove you wrong.

**Think about it**
Is there a question in your research that might be nudging people toward the answer you want?

**Use this in your project**
Review your survey or interview questions and flag any that sound leading.

## 3. Data Privacy Basics

**What is it?**
Data privacy means being thoughtful and careful about what personal information you collect from people, and making sure you only use it for the reason you said you would.

**Why does it matter?**
People trust you when they share information with you. If that information is shared carelessly, used for something else, or collected without a good reason, it damages that trust — and could actually harm someone.

**Example**
You're only researching a lunch-line problem, but your survey also asks for students' full names, grades, and home addresses — information you don't actually need.

**Common mistake**
Collecting more personal information than the project actually requires, "just in case."

**Try this**
Look at your survey or interview questions and remove anything that isn't necessary to understand the problem.

**Key takeaway**
Only collect what you actually need, and protect it once you have it.

**Think about it**
If someone asked why you needed a specific piece of personal information, could you give a clear answer?

**Use this in your project**
Check your data collection tools for any unnecessary personal questions before using them.

## 4. Research Integrity

**What is it?**
Research integrity means being honest about what your research actually shows — including results that don't support your idea — instead of only reporting the parts that make your idea look good.

**Why does it matter?**
If you quietly ignore evidence that contradicts your idea, you're not really doing research anymore — you're just looking for reasons to agree with yourself. That leads to weaker projects and less trust from reviewers.

**Example**
Three interviews support your idea, but one person raises a real concern. Leaving that concern out of your notes makes your research look stronger than it actually is.

**Common mistake**
Reporting only the evidence that supports your idea and quietly dropping the rest.

**Try this**
Look back at your research notes. Is there anything you left out because it didn't support your idea? Add it back in.

**Key takeaway**
Report what you found — not just what you hoped to find.

**Think about it**
Have you ever noticed evidence that challenged your idea, and been tempted to ignore it?

**Use this in your project**
Include at least one piece of evidence in your Research Brief that complicates or challenges your idea.

## 5. Source Triangulation

**What is it?**
Source triangulation means checking a claim using more than one type of evidence — for example, an interview, a survey, and an article — instead of relying on just one source.

**Why does it matter?**
One survey, one article, or one interview is rarely enough to be sure something is true. Different sources can confirm each other, or reveal that your first source was incomplete or misleading.

**Example**
An interview suggests students skip breakfast because they're rushed. A survey confirms this is common. A school schedule shows why: first period starts right after buses arrive, leaving no time.

**Common mistake**
Treating a single interview or article as if it settles the question.

**Try this**
Take one claim from your research and find a second, different type of source that either supports or challenges it.

**Key takeaway**
One source is a clue. Multiple sources are evidence.

**Think about it**
Is there a key claim in your project that's currently only backed by one source?

**Use this in your project**
Confirm your most important research finding using at least two different types of sources.

## 6. Citation vs. Plagiarism

**What is it?**
Citation means giving credit to where information, ideas, or data came from. Plagiarism is using someone else's work or words without giving that credit — whether or not it was on purpose.

**Why does it matter?**
Giving credit isn't just a formatting rule — it's about honesty. It shows reviewers what's actually your thinking versus what you learned from someone else, and it respects the original source's work.

**Example**
You read a great explanation of a problem in an article. Using that idea is fine — as long as you say where it came from instead of presenting it as your own original thought.

**Common mistake**
Copying a sentence or idea closely from a source and presenting it as your own without any credit.

**Try this**
Go through your Research Brief and make sure every fact, quote, or idea that came from somewhere else has a source attached.

**Key takeaway**
Credit isn't optional — it's part of being a trustworthy researcher.

**Think about it**
Would you be comfortable if the original source of your information saw exactly how you used it?

**Use this in your project**
Add a simple source list to your Research Brief for every fact or idea you didn't come up with yourself.

**Credible Sources — quick reference**
- *Usually stronger:* government sources, universities, peer-reviewed research, established nonprofits, recognized industry sources, direct expert interviews
- *Use carefully:* blogs, social media, forums, AI-generated summaries
- *Always ask:* Who created this? Why? What evidence do they provide?

## 7. Problem Ecosystem / System Mapping *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
System mapping means looking at a problem as part of a bigger picture — identifying the different people, causes, and factors that interact with each other to create it.

**Why does it matter?**
Problems rarely have one single cause. If you only look at the most obvious cause, you might build a solution that misses the real issue — or accidentally makes a different part of the system worse.

**Example**
Students being late to class isn't just about a crowded hallway — it also involves class schedules, locker locations, and how many students share one narrow path.

**Common mistake**
Assuming a problem has one simple cause when multiple factors are actually contributing.

**Try this**
Draw a simple map of your problem: list every person, place, and factor connected to it, and draw lines showing how they affect each other.

**Key takeaway**
Most real problems are systems, not single causes.

**Think about it**
Is there a factor influencing your problem that you haven't considered yet?

**Use this in your project**
Add your system map to your Research Brief alongside your root cause analysis.

---

## Closing Reflection for Stage 2
**Good research can change your mind.**

Before moving to Stage 3 (IMAGINE), a student should be able to show multiple types of evidence, honestly explain what the research found, identify a credible root cause, and explain what existing solutions currently miss.
