# i3League — Stage 3: IMAGINE
### Guiding Question: What could solve it, which direction should we choose, and what must we test first?

---

## 1. Value Proposition

**What is it?**
A value proposition is a clear, simple statement of why someone would actually choose and use your solution — what makes it worth their time, money, or effort.

**Why does it matter?**
An idea can be clever and still fail if nobody has a real reason to use it. Naming the value clearly forces you to think from the user's side, not just the builder's side.

**Example**
Not: "An app that shows hallway congestion." Instead: "A way for students to know which hallway route saves them time, so they're not late to class."

**Common mistake**
Describing what the solution *does* instead of why someone would actually *want* it.

**Try this**
Finish this sentence for your idea: "Someone would use this because ___."

**Key takeaway**
A good idea explains why someone would choose it, not just what it does.

**Think about it**
If you were the user, would you actually choose this — or just say it sounds nice?

**Use this in your project**
Write a one-sentence value proposition and include it in your Concept Portfolio.

---

## 2. User Needs vs. Features

**What is it?**
A need is the underlying thing a person is trying to accomplish. A feature is a specific tool or function you build. Features should exist to serve needs — not the other way around.

**Why does it matter?**
It's easy to get excited about adding features because they seem impressive, even if they don't actually help the person you're designing for.

**Example**
The need: "I want to know if the hallway is crowded before I get there." A feature that serves it: a simple color-coded status. An unnecessary feature: a full social media profile for hallway check-ins.

**Common mistake**
Adding a feature because it sounds advanced, not because it serves a real need you identified in Stage 1 or 2.

**Try this**
List every feature in your idea. Next to each, write which specific need from your research it actually serves.

**Key takeaway**
Every feature should trace back to a real need.

**Think about it**
Is there a feature in your idea that exists mostly because it sounds impressive?

**Use this in your project**
Cut or flag any feature in your Concept Portfolio that doesn't clearly connect to a need from your research.

---

## 3. Divergent Thinking / Solution Exploration

**What is it?**
Divergent thinking means deliberately coming up with several genuinely different ways to solve a problem before deciding which one is best — instead of settling on the first idea that comes to mind.

**Why does it matter?**
The first idea you think of is rarely the strongest one. Generating multiple different approaches gives you real options to compare, instead of just one idea to defend.

**Example**
For the hallway congestion problem: a scheduling change, a new hallway route, a staggered release time, and a simple crowd-alert system — four genuinely different directions, not four versions of the same idea.

**Common mistake**
Generating three ideas that are really just small variations of the same underlying concept.

**Try this**
Brainstorm at least three solutions to your problem that work in completely different ways from each other.

**Key takeaway**
Compare real options — don't just polish your first idea.

**Think about it**
Are your current ideas actually different from each other, or just small tweaks on one idea?

**Use this in your project**
Include at least three genuinely different concepts in your Concept Portfolio.

---

## 4. Constraints Thinking

**What is it?**
Constraints are the real-world limits your solution has to work within — like budget, time, available technology, school policy, or your team's current skills.

**Why does it matter?**
An idea that ignores real constraints might sound great but be impossible to actually build or use. Designing with constraints in mind from the start saves you from a painful redesign later.

**Example**
An idea that requires every student to install a new app may not be realistic if the school doesn't allow personal devices during passing periods.

**Common mistake**
Designing your "dream version" first and only discovering the real-world limits after you've already committed to the idea.

**Try this**
List your project's real constraints — time, budget, tech, policy, skills — and check whether your idea actually fits within them.

**Key takeaway**
The best ideas work inside real limits, not around them.

**Think about it**
What's the biggest constraint your idea currently ignores?

**Use this in your project**
Add a constraints list to your Concept Portfolio next to your chosen concept.

---

## 5. Risk Identification

**What is it?**
Risk identification means thinking ahead about what could realistically go wrong with your idea — technically, socially, or practically — before you've invested a lot of time building it.

**Why does it matter?**
Every idea has weak points. Spotting them early means you can plan around them, instead of being surprised by them later when they're harder to fix.

**Example**
A hallway-alert idea might fail if students simply ignore the alerts, or if the sensor data isn't accurate enough to trust.

**Common mistake**
Only thinking about why an idea will work, and never seriously asking how it might fail.

**Try this**
List the top two ways your idea could realistically fail, and one idea for how you'd catch that early.

**Key takeaway**
Naming a risk early is how you get ahead of it.

**Think about it**
What would have to be true for your idea to fail?

**Use this in your project**
Add your top risks to your Concept Portfolio, next to your selected concept.

---

## 6. Accessibility & Inclusion

**What is it?**
Accessibility and inclusion mean making sure your solution actually works for the full range of people who might use it — including people with disabilities, different backgrounds, or different levels of access to technology.

**Why does it matter?**
A solution that only works for some of the people who need it isn't fully solving the problem. Thinking about this early is much easier than trying to fix it after the solution is built.

**Example**
A hallway-alert app that relies only on a phone screen might exclude a student who is visually impaired or doesn't have a personal device.

**Common mistake**
Designing only around the "typical" user and treating accessibility as an afterthought, if it's considered at all.

**Try this**
List one group of people who might be left out by your current idea, and one way you could adjust for them.

**Key takeaway**
If it doesn't work for everyone affected, it isn't finished yet.

**Think about it**
Who might struggle to use your idea, even if most people wouldn't?

**Use this in your project**
Note at least one accessibility consideration in your Concept Portfolio.

---

## 7. Responsible / Ethical Design

**What is it?**
Responsible design means thinking about how your solution could affect people beyond its intended use — including ways it could be misused, feel unfair, or create new problems.

**Why does it matter?**
Good intentions aren't the same as good outcomes. Innovators are responsible for thinking through how their solution behaves in the real world, not just in the best-case scenario.

**Example**
A system that tracks which hallways students use could unintentionally reveal private information about someone's schedule or habits.

**Common mistake**
Only evaluating an idea by whether it solves the problem, without asking who else it might affect and how.

**Try this**
Ask: could this solution be used in a way I didn't intend? Could it treat any group unfairly?

**Key takeaway**
A responsible innovator thinks past the best-case scenario.

**Think about it**
Is there a way your idea could be misused, even if that's not what you intend?

**Use this in your project**
Add one ethical consideration to your Concept Portfolio for your selected concept.

---

## 8. AI Fit & Responsible Use

**What is it?**
This is a deliberate check on whether your problem actually needs AI to solve it — and if it does, what that AI use requires and risks.

**Why does it matter?**
AI is a powerful tool, but it isn't always the right one. Adding AI because it sounds impressive, without a real reason, can make a solution more complicated, less trustworthy, or unnecessarily risky.

**Example**
A hallway congestion problem might be solved with a simple schedule change — no AI required. A more complex prediction of daily crowd patterns might genuinely benefit from it.

**Common mistake**
Adding AI to a project because it sounds advanced, without asking what it actually adds.

**Try this**
Answer four questions about your idea: What does AI add? What could go wrong? What data would it require? Could a simpler solution work better?

**Key takeaway**
AI should earn its place in your solution — not be assumed.

**Think about it**
If you removed AI from your idea entirely, would it still mostly work?

**Use this in your project**
Answer the four AI Fit questions in your Concept Portfolio for any concept that uses AI.

---

## 9. Assumption Prioritization

**What is it?**
Assumption prioritization means identifying the beliefs your idea depends on being true — and figuring out which one, if wrong, would completely break the idea.

**Why does it matter?**
Every idea rests on assumptions. Some are minor. One or two are usually critical — if those turn out to be false, the whole idea falls apart. Testing those first saves time.

**Example**
A hallway-alert idea assumes students will actually look at and trust the alert. If that assumption is wrong, the rest of the idea doesn't matter.

**Common mistake**
Testing an easy, low-stakes assumption first instead of the one that could actually sink the idea.

**Try this**
List your idea's assumptions, then circle the one that would hurt the most if it turned out to be false.

**Key takeaway**
Find the assumption that could kill your idea, and test that one first.

**Think about it**
What are you hoping is true about your idea that you haven't actually confirmed yet?

**Use this in your project**
Name your Critical Assumption in your Concept Portfolio — this becomes your first test in ITERATE.

## 10. Build vs. Buy vs. Partner *(HS CORE)*

**What is it?**
This is the decision of whether to build a solution yourself from scratch, use an existing tool or platform, or team up with someone who already has part of what you need.

**Why does it matter?**
Not every good idea needs to be built entirely from the ground up. Sometimes the smartest path to real impact is using or adapting something that already exists.

**Example**
Instead of building a custom scheduling system, a team could adapt a free existing scheduling tool and focus their effort on the parts that are actually new.

**Common mistake**
Assuming that building everything yourself is automatically more impressive or more valid than using existing tools.

**Try this**
For your idea, list what already exists that you could use or adapt, versus what genuinely needs to be built new.

**Key takeaway**
The goal is impact, not reinventing what already works.

**Think about it**
Is there a part of your idea you're planning to build that already exists somewhere else?

**Use this in your project**
Note your build/buy/partner decision and reasoning in your Concept Portfolio.

---

## 11. Originality, Attribution & IP

**What is it?**
This means being clear about what parts of your idea are genuinely original, what parts are inspired by or adapted from existing work, and making sure you give credit where it's due.

**Why does it matter?**
Almost no idea is 100% new — most build on something that came before. That's normal. What matters is being honest about it, so your project is credible and respectful of other people's work.

**Example**
Your alert-system concept might be inspired by traffic-light apps used elsewhere — adapted for a school hallway. That's a legitimate starting point, as long as it's disclosed.

**Common mistake**
Presenting an adapted idea as if it were entirely original, without acknowledging what it was based on.

**Try this**
List anything your concept was inspired by or adapted from, and note what you changed or added.

**Key takeaway**
Build on existing ideas honestly — don't disguise them as entirely new.

**Think about it**
Is there a part of your idea that's more "adapted" than "original," and have you said so clearly?

**Use this in your project**
Document what inspired your concept, what already exists, what you adapted, what you created, and anything that may need attribution or further IP guidance. Include this in your Concept Portfolio.

---

## 12. Decision Matrix / Concept Selection

**What is it?**
A decision matrix is a simple tool for comparing your different concepts side by side against the same criteria — like impact, feasibility, and cost — so your final choice is reasoned, not just a gut feeling.

**Why does it matter?**
When you've generated multiple real options, you need a fair way to choose between them. A decision matrix makes your reasoning visible — to yourself and to reviewers.

**Example**
Comparing three concepts against criteria like "solves the real problem," "feasible with our resources," and "affects the most people" — scoring each, and seeing which concept comes out ahead.

**Common mistake**
Picking the idea you personally like best and skipping a fair side-by-side comparison of the alternatives.

**Try this**
List your concepts down one side and 3–4 criteria across the top. Score each concept on each criterion.

**Key takeaway**
Choose with a method, not just a feeling.

**Think about it**
Would your decision change if you were more honest about your scores?

**Use this in your project**
Include your completed decision matrix in your Concept Portfolio.

---

## Closing Reflection for Stage 3
**What could solve it, which direction should we choose, and what must we test first?**

Before moving to Stage 4 (ITERATE), a student should be able to show multiple genuinely different concepts, a reasoned selection using a decision matrix, a named critical assumption, and an honest account of what already exists, what was adapted or credited, and what is distinct about the selected concept.
